\lettrine{L}{egend of the Technomancers} is played in sessions: stretches of time
where the GM sets the world in motion and the players decide what to do
about it. This part of the book covers what happens \emph{during} play ---
how conflicts resolve, how the world responds, and how a party travels
from one realm to the next.

\section{Degrees of Success}
% REAL RULE, 2026-09-22 design notes: tie = partial, beat = success,
% more 6s than the target = critical (overrides the tiers below it).
Once you have rolled your pool and counted successes --- each die showing
\dice{4}, \dice{5}, or \dice{6} --- compare the total to the target number
the GM set before you rolled.

\begin{statblock}[Reading the Result]
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{0.36\linewidth} Y@{}}
Fewer successes than the target & \keyword{Failure} --- it does not go your way.\\[3pt]
Successes equal to the target & \keyword{Partial Success} --- you get what you wanted, but at a cost or complication.\\[3pt]
More successes than the target & \keyword{Success} --- it goes exactly as you hoped.\\[3pt]
\dice{6}s rolled outnumber the target & \keyword{Critical Success} --- overrides every tier above.\\
\end{tabularx}
\end{statblock}

Check for a critical first: if the number of dice showing \dice{6} is
\emph{greater than} the target, that critical result stands no matter
what your total success count was. Otherwise, read the result off your
total successes as above.

\begin{example}
Rooster forces a jammed blast door with \keyword{Body} \dice{2} and
\atrtag{skill}{Athletics} --- \dice{3} dice against a target of \dice{1}.
He rolls \dice{6}, \dice{6}, \dice{3}. Two dice came up \dice{4} or
better --- two successes, more than the target, an ordinary
\keyword{Success}. But two of those dice show a \dice{6}, and two is also
more than the target of \dice{1}. The critical check wins: Rooster gets a
\keyword{Critical Success} instead --- the door doesn't just open, it
comes off its hinges.
\end{example}

\section{Conflicts}
Fights, chases, arguments, and schemes all follow the same rhythm. The GM
describes the situation and what threatens the Travellers. A player says
what their Traveller wants to accomplish and what they do to accomplish
it. There is no list of combat actions to choose from, no initiative
order, and no separate turn for an enemy to attack. If an action is
impossible, say why; if its outcome is certain, let it happen. Roll only
when both the outcome and the consequences are in doubt.

Before the roll, the GM makes the stakes clear: what is at risk and what
the target number is. The player chooses a base \keyword{Stat} for their
action and rolls it plus applicable tags, as in \emph{How to Play}. Only
players roll. After the result, describe what
changes in the scene, then pass the spotlight to whoever is in a
position to act. A failed roll changes the situation; it never just
wastes a turn.

\subsection*{Taking a Track Down}
An action can wear down an opponent's \keyword{Body}, \keyword{Mind}, or
\keyword{Face} track. Choose the track by what the action actually does:
a blow or a shove threatens Body, a ruse or a puzzle threatens Mind,
and a public rebuke or a challenge to someone's authority threatens
Face. The track being threatened need not match the Stat the player
rolls. A clever trap might roll Mind to hurt a foe's Body; a terrifying
display of strength might roll Body to shake their Face.

In an ordinary exchange, a clean success takes \dice{1} point from the
opponent's chosen track. On a failure, the opponent holds their ground
and the PC usually loses \dice{1} point from the track the GM's
response threatens. Often that is the Stat the PC rolled, but follow
the fiction: a failed Mind roll to spring a trap might leave the PC
physically exposed instead. On a partial success, the PC gets what they
wanted, including \dice{1} point of track loss if that was the aim, but
the GM adds a cost: a point from a PC track, a lost position, a new
threat, or another complication. A critical success can do more than
take a point --- settle a lesser threat outright or create a decisive
opening, as the fiction allows. Bigger losses are possible when the
stakes warrant them; the GM should establish that danger before the
roll, not discover it afterward.

\begin{example}
Mara rushes an ogre with her sword. The GM warns that its club is ready
to meet her and calls for a Body roll. On a success, the ogre loses
\dice{1} Body; on a miss, Mara takes \dice{1} Body instead. Later, when
the ogre guards a bridge, Mara tries to lure it away by claiming its
master has called for help. That is a Mind roll against the ogre's Mind
track. If it has just \dice{1} Mind, a success sends it away from the
bridge; running it through would not have done the same thing.
\end{example}

The GM can advance the opposition whenever the fiction calls for it:
show a new danger, have a foe press an advantage, or follow through on
a threat the players leave unanswered. The GM does not wait for a failed
roll or an enemy's turn to make the world move. Give the players a clear
picture of what is happening and a chance to respond when they can;
when they cannot, follow through with the established consequence.
